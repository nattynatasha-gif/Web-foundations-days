# Day 8 Reflection

The hardest concept in the course for me was designing under extreme concurrency while still guaranteeing correctness. I could design happy-path flows easily, but understanding what happens when 200 000 people hit the same 20 000 seats in ten minutes was difficult. Race conditions, lock contention, and the difference between optimistic and pessimistic approaches felt abstract until I worked through the exact sequence of SELECT … FOR UPDATE → status change → commit. Once I drew the timeline of two users trying to buy the same seat, the need for transactions, unique constraints, and short-lived holds became clear. That concrete example is what finally made the theory click.

Based on the feedback I received on earlier work, the biggest improvement I would make to this capstone is stronger non-functional requirements and clearer failure handling. Previously I listed latency targets without justifying them or explaining what the system does when the payment provider is slow or a hold expires. In a revised version I would add explicit SLOs, describe the waiting-room behaviour, and show how the system degrades gracefully instead of failing completely.

Next I want to go deeper into the practical side of the patterns we studied: Redis for inventory holds, PostgreSQL locking under load, and building a simple waiting-room prototype. Implementing even a small version of the hold-and-purchase flow will turn the theoretical guarantees into something I can actually see and debug.
